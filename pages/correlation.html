Correlation

Ten measured correlations with bootstrap confidence intervals, plus the correlation arithmetic that actually prices a parlay.

Correlation values, measured rather than asserted. Ten relationships across all 32 clubs, each with a bootstrap confidence interval, plus a second set showing how much correlation moves a parlay's true value.

Read the scope before the numbers. These are cross-sectional correlations: 32 clubs, one snapshot. They answer "when a team is better at X, is it also better at Y?" They do not answer "if this leg wins, how does the other leg's probability move?" — which is the only correlation that prices a parlay. The two are different quantities and are not interchangeable. No same-game prop correlation is claimed anywhere in this repository, because it cannot be computed from team-level data.

What the numbers actually say

  • Efficiency metrics are strong team ratings. Offensive EPA/play explains 85.8% of the variance in points scored. That is excellent for rating a team and useless as an edge, because everyone can see it.
  • Rushing efficiency is a trap. r = 0.023, CI including zero, R² of 0.1%. Any strategy built on rushing yards per attempt was killed by this row before it cost anything. It is kept on the strategy board as a rejected item so it does not get rebuilt.
  • Pressure matters more than volume. Sacks taken vs points scored: r = -0.621 (R² 38.6%). Defensive interceptions vs points allowed: r = -0.561. Both are real and both are widely known, which is exactly why neither is likely to be an edge on its own.
  • Pass volume beats pass efficiency in one direction only. Total passing yards vs PPG is r = 0.595, weaker than yards per attempt at 0.685. Efficiency, not volume — a useful caution for prop markets, where volume is what the line is set on.

The correlation that prices a parlay

Two legs, each modelled as a no-vig 50/50, priced together at −110 / −110 (decimal 3.6446). The latent correlation between the legs is ρ. This is a bivariate-normal copula; at ρ = 0 it must reproduce 25.00%, and it does.

The decision this forces. The same two legs swing from -32.75% to 14.99% EV purely on ρ. Any model that assumes independence is not approximating — it is answering a different question. This is why Gate G7 blocks multi-leg strategies until ρ is estimated from real data.

Why we cannot yet compute the prop correlations that matter

The parlay cases in the brief — QB passing yards with his WR1's receiving yards, or a rusher with the opposing quarterback — require event-level data: both players' outcomes in the same game, repeated over hundreds of games. This repository contains team totals and player season totals. Neither can produce a joint distribution. What is needed:

  1. Player game logs for both legs, at least three seasons (to avoid a one-season fluke).
  2. Consistent player identifiers across sources, so legs can be joined reliably.
  3. Prop lines as offered, so the correlation can be turned into a price comparison rather than an academic statistic.

This is recorded as Q07 and as suggestion SG06.

Inputsdata/team-metrics-2026-w10.csv (32 clubs)
EstimatorPearson product-moment correlation
IntervalPercentile bootstrap, 4,000 resamples, seed 7 — reproducible
ReadA CI that includes zero means the data cannot distinguish the effect from no effect, and it is reported as such.
Not doneNo p-hacking, no subgroup mining, no per-team correlations. With n = 32, per-team analysis would be noise.
